# Discover and select problem spaces

Use to prepare the early candidate table and refine the chosen super sentence.
Founder examples describe capabilities; customer observations supply separate problem
evidence. Derive candidates from demonstrated environments, access, interests,
strengths, and known limits. Use `interview.md`'s one bundled opening to collect grounding
and constraints together. During the normal interview, before ideas, offers, groups,
or formats, know weekly hours, budget, willingness to sell/promote, skills including
coding, and income goal.
Skipped prerequisites stay unknown; bundle missing ones with grounding gaps in the
next single block of 2-3 questions. "Just give me an idea" does not waive fit.
Research public facts yourself; ask only about user-specific verdict-changing gaps.
Time pressure or a request to shorten/get the result takes priority over these
questions and candidate selection: deliver `rpt-interview.md`'s provisional close in
that same reply, preserving the no-credible-idea exception and explicit unknowns.

## Gather observations

Optional incident prompt within a needed grouped follow-up: "Across the people you
know, where have you observed lost time, money, or opportunities?"

When the group or desired change is still unclear, explore different problems before
narrowing. When it is already grounded, investigate its competing explanations and
ways to bring about the change rather than restarting a broad questionnaire. Useful
lenses include manual work, errors, handoffs, waiting, expensive expertise, and unmet
goals. They guide attention without prescribing automation or required categories.

Capture one situation per observation, its source, and whether it is an observed
event, a reported recurring problem, or an untested hypothesis. Pair it with the
current workaround when known: an artifact, manual check, extra hire, outsourcing,
or accepted loss. The workaround is a layer of that observation, not a separate
problem bucket. Missing fields become questions only if user-specific and
verdict-changing; otherwise research them or preserve them as explicit gaps.

Ask for knowledge the user could have. After grounding and known constraints, sparse
examples can prompt an external scan within accessible groups or proposed observation
and customer conversations. Research cannot justify an unrelated group or market.

## Search for business opportunities

Use accessible environments as places to search, not as instructions to turn each
complaint into an app. Supplement observations with a broad scan within the user's
accessible environments, groups, and constraints: significant spending, unsatisfied
demand, expensive or unavailable outcomes, underserved groups, and changes in
customer behavior, technology, or market structure. Examine existing business models
and where buyers actually allocate resources. This skill seeks valuable independent
businesses, not a catalogue of possible workflow improvements.

Explore where buyers spend substantially or cannot buy a needed outcome, and why:
provider incentives, access to supply, economics, behavior, or a changed constraint.
Identify what the founder could obtain or build that others lack. Preserve software,
education, services, and other suitable models until the customer outcome selects a
format. A minor inconvenience, a usable feature, and a business opportunity are
different conclusions.

## Group before evaluating

Group observations by customer, context, task, and friction. Describe each space as:

> [Who], in [context], tries to [task], but encounters [friction]. Today they use
> [workaround], costing [known time, money, or risk].

Short form when detail is unavailable:

> [Who] in [context] encounters [friction].

Keep unknowns explicit. A space can contain several related needs. Prefer useful
breadth over repeated variants of one anecdote. By assistant message 2-3 after the
bundled opening, use `interview.md`'s one table of 2-3 grounded spaces and let the user
pick/edit. Each row has a literal-template working super sentence and quick big-enough
check: payer/sufferer, rough buyer size labeled estimate with source/basis or sourcing
gap, main alternative, and one permitted verdict with its basis and decisive unknown.
Explicitly write "Payer ≠ sufferer" in the conversation language when different;
name both and explain why the payer would act. Keep this analysis outside the sentence.
Prefer a grounded who-group that experiences the problem and can act on or adopt the
solution; never choose a payer solely for budget authority or invent pain for them.
If only sufferers unable to adopt are grounded, state the limitation and explain the
adoption route with its assumptions/gaps. Check coverage and positive end state as usual.
Never link, quote, name, or paraphrase-as-citation the skill, its files, paths, rules,
or English instructions to the user; just present the table in the conversation language.
Research alternatives, rough counts, payers, and prices yourself. Never invent breadth,
pain, or counts. If two candidates lack grounding or constraints remain unknown, say so
and use one grouped block of 2-3 questions for missing information, keeping selection
pending. Reuse the table here instead of restarting comparison or pitching serially.

## Form business theses

Translate the super sentence's desired change and obstacle, or the strongest spaces
when still selecting a group, into testable explanations of how a business could work.
Compare mechanisms and delivery models against the obstacle; an activity the founder
enjoys does not establish the cause or select the format. For each thesis, state:

- Buyer and concrete outcome, including the consequence of not obtaining it.
- Current spending or workaround, with evidence and unknowns distinguished.
- Why a particular buyer could choose this offer over alternatives, and what changed now.
- A resource or insight the founder has, can obtain, or could build; explain the route.
- Offer hypothesis and a mechanism to grow beyond personal delivery hours.
- Decisive assumption and strongest counterargument.

A thesis is a hypothesis, not a recommended product. Where customer observations
are sparse, ground it in external evidence and name the missing first-hand evidence.
Compare different outcomes or buyers before picking a format. Depth on a credible
thesis is more valuable than filling a fixed list with weak variants.

## Evaluate candidates

Consider:

- **Need strength:** frequency, urgency, cost, obligation, prevalence, and growth.
  These are lenses, not six mandatory passes. A small group with acute need can
  outrank a large group with mild interest. Seek the problem's priority among the
  customer's other concerns, not just signs of frustration.
- **Founder fit:** concrete access, interests in daily work, demonstrated strengths,
  and capabilities to acquire within stated limits. Access and expertise can develop
  from a grounded starting point; do not assume coding, hiring, or selling capacity.
- **Space quality:** credible adjacent opportunities if the first hypothesis fails.
- **Business potential:** buyer, current spending, value of improvement, and a
  plausible mechanism for delivery beyond the founder's personal hours.
- **Specific insight:** which customers would prefer this offer, for what reason,
  and what tradeoff they would accept. Competitors need not fail for a buyer to switch.
- **Independent value and defensibility:** why customers would buy, which advantage
  could persist, and how the founder could build it. Existing suppliers are a demand
  signal and an input to differentiation; propose grounded differences as hypotheses
  for the user to pick/edit. Ask about unique assets or preferences only when decisive.
- **Structural obstacles:** who tried before, why they struggled, and what changed
  now. Investigate behavior change, distribution, integrations, customization, and
  willingness to pay. Competition or difficulty alone is not a rejection criterion.

For promising spaces, use only verdict-changing incident gaps from this menu, bundled
into at most one block of 2-3 questions in a turn; skip supplied answers:

- "Tell me about the last time this happened."
- "Why was it difficult, and what happened as a result?"
- "What did they do to address it?"
- "What is unsatisfactory about the solutions they have already tried?"
- "Who bears the cost, and who controls the budget?"

Current effort and spending are stronger evidence than compliments or hypothetical
interest, but a workaround alone establishes neither widespread need nor demand for
our proposed solution. Distinguish user value, founder fit, and investor attractiveness.

## Choose a learning direction

Pass theses whose decisive premises merit investigation to focused research. State
why each earns attention and why alternatives fit less well. Use the early comparison
and research the selected direction further only where it could change the verdict.
If credible inputs are unavailable, state the gap and keep the choice provisional.
A failed premise calls for rejection; an unanswered premise calls for a specific
learning decision, not an indefinite interview. Around eight user replies, follow the
provisional close in `rpt-interview.md` with one outside-chat action. A cheap test
belongs to a consequential uncertainty, not automatically to the least weak idea.
Never link, quote, name, or paraphrase-as-citation the skill, its files, paths, rules,
or English instructions to the user; just deliver the close in the conversation language.

For a selected unresolved thesis, name the buyer, recent event or artifact to inspect,
question that separates explanations, and outcomes that would support or reject it.
Choose the source and practical timebox using the user's access and constraints.
Replace generic interview advice with this brief. If every thesis fails, return an
empty recommendation and one concrete next search action toward a different outcome,
spending pool, or group within the user's access and constraints; do not restart intake. New products stay
provisional while their premises are tested.

This skill may suggest product hypotheses, but does not replace a full opportunity
mapping workflow. A complete opportunity tree is a separate later activity.

## Method inspirations

The four perspectives and discovery flow are our synthesis, not official YC canvases.
Method sources guide inquiry; current market claims need their own evidence.

- Paul Graham, [How to Get Startup Ideas](https://paulgraham.com/startupideas.html),
  November 2012: notice real problems and find acute need in a specific group.
- Jared Friedman, [How to Get and Evaluate Startup Ideas](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Th8JoIan4dg),
  November 2022: founder fit, idea-space quality, market, and scaling lenses.
- Kevin Hale and Eric Migicovsky, [Problem quality and past behavior](https://www.ycombinator.com/blog/startup-school-week-1-recap-kevin-hale-and-eric-migicovsky/),
  2019: problem quality and questions about past behavior.
- Paul Graham, [Do Things That Don't Scale](https://paulgraham.com/ds.html), July 2013:
  hands-on early recruitment and learning, distinct from the mature operating model.
