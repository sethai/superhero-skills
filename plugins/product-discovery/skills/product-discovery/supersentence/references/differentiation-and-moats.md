# Differentiation and paths to moats

Use after identifying a customer outcome and relevant competitors. This is a practical
menu for the interview, not a scoring taxonomy or a requirement to invent a moat.
Research public facts yourself. Use this menu only for verdict-changing gaps; ask
about user-specific assets, incidents, or preferences in at most one grouped block
of 2-3 questions per turn. Do not create a separate moat interview or confirmation loop.

## Start with the intended difference

Competing businesses with paying customers are a positive demand signal. Establish
what customers already buy through your own research, then propose plausible,
grounded differences as labeled hypotheses for the user to pick/edit. Ask what only
they know about assets or preferred tradeoffs if it could change the verdict; do not
require an unaided differentiation answer before helping.

Make the answer concrete: which buyer would prefer it, what would improve for them,
and what tradeoff would they accept? When needed, offer a few directions based on
demonstrated access, interests, strengths, and limits, checking constraints before
suggesting: a particular customer, a more useful outcome, access, reliability,
speed, convenience, service scope, price, or a different business model. Each can
be a valid starting wedge without being a moat. A clear niche or lower price becomes
more defensible only through a specific asset, cost structure, capability, or loop.

## Catalogue of mechanisms

| Mechanism | A difference customers might value | How it can become durable | Useful question or evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Distribution access | Reaching relevant buyers through a trusted audience, partner, or channel | Repeatable access or privileged partnerships make acquisition materially harder or costlier for a rival | Which buyers can you reach, on what terms, and with what demonstrated response? |
| Scarce supply and partnerships | Access to people, capacity, inventory, locations, or services when needed | Secured availability, reliable partner relationships, or preferential access take time and investment to reproduce | What can you actually secure, at what cost, and why would suppliers keep participating? |
| Useful proprietary data | Better decisions or results from information others lack | Lawfully usable, refreshed data improve outcomes and attract additional useful observations | Which data, what use rights, what improvement, and how can a rival obtain an equivalent? |
| Specialist know-how, technology, and process | Consistently solving a difficult problem better | Technical capability, tacit team knowledge, reusable methods, or protected IP take substantial effort to reproduce | What result can the organization repeat beyond the founder, and what would copying require? |
| Economies of scale or local density | Better availability or a lower sustainable price | Volume lowers unit cost or improves capacity use while preserving quality | How do costs change with volume, including coordination, unused capacity, and expansion? |
| Network effects | Better matching, choice, or collaboration as relevant participants join | Each additional participant improves value for others, supporting retention and further participation | Who benefits when another participant joins, and why is this more than a larger directory? |
| Workflow embedding and switching costs | Less coordination or rework through accumulated context and shared work | Valuable history, routines, integrations, and team adoption make replacing the service costly | What real customer value accumulates, and what must be rebuilt when switching? |
| Brand and earned trust | Confidence in a consequential purchase | Verifiable outcomes, reputation, referrals, and consistent delivery compound over time | Who already trusts you for this purchase, what earned that trust, and can a team maintain it? |
| Business model competitors struggle to copy | A purchasing or delivery model better suited to a buyer | Matching it would damage a rival's economics, channel relationships, or core business | What specifically makes copying unattractive, rather than merely possible but not yet done? |

These mechanisms can reinforce one another. Select the few grounded in the user's
resources and goals. Specialist positioning, a pleasant interface, AI, fast launch,
or a subscription are starting choices; explain the underlying mechanism before
calling them durable. A business may have meaningful advantages without becoming
winner-take-all or meeting venture-scale expectations.

## Develop the path instead of demanding a finished moat

For a relevant difference, assess these dimensions from existing answers and research;
they are decision inputs, not five required user questions:

1. **Customer value now:** buyer, benefit, comparison, and accepted tradeoff.
2. **Starting position:** asset or capability already present, with evidence and limits.
3. **Build path:** what can be obtained or accumulated, first acquisition step, cost,
   and dependencies. Contacts and supplier willingness are different from commitments.
4. **Durability:** why copying is hard, slow, uneconomic, or does not remove our value.
5. **Failure condition:** what observation would defeat that mechanism.

If decisive user-specific gaps remain, bundle ordinary follow-ups such as "What would
make that difficult to reproduce?" and "How could you build that advantage from your
current position?" Distinguish user
statements, research, and proposed hypotheses. When the user lacks an answer, help
form a plausible path or mark the question unresolved; absence of an established
moat at the start is not itself a reason to abandon the business. Respect the roughly
eight-reply soft limit; unresolved advantage can become the outside-chat test rather
than another questioning cycle.

Competition and copyability inform the choice; they do not replace exploration of
why this customer would choose this business. A positive recommendation still needs
a credible customer benefit, buying route, and delivery model. Preserve a justified
negative outcome when those premises fail after exploration.
