# Experiment Catalog

These families are a practical, non-exhaustive selection aid. Start with an assumption and behavior that could contradict it. Families may overlap.

## Select by question

| Question | Useful starting methods | Necessary distinction |
|---|---|---|
| Does the offer attract the intended audience? | Ad smoke test, fake door, landing page | Exposure, click and qualified request are separate events |
| Can people understand and complete the task? | Paper/clickable prototype, Wizard of Oz | Observe behavior rather than asking whether they would use it |
| Does delivery help in their actual situation? | Concierge, Wizard of Oz, thin functional prototype | Record a useful outcome, not only completion or praise |
| Do they return when the need recurs? | Repeated manual/functional delivery, renewal | Allow another genuine opportunity to use it |
| Will a buyer commit at these terms? | Priced offer with LOI, deposit, presale or paid pilot | Identify budget authority; response depends on price and terms |
| Can technology meet requirements? | Technical spike, benchmark, thin functional prototype | Human delivery cannot establish this |
| Can delivery sustain the business? | Costed delivery pilot plus price/cost evidence | Sales alone do not establish viable unit economics |

A/B allocation, before/after comparisons and counterbalanced tasks are study designs, not families. The family does not by itself establish causality.

## 1. Prototypes

| Subtype | Setup / observed signal | Does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| Paper sketches or storyboard | Screens/cards with a task; observe interpretation, choices, missing information | Working interactions, live adoption or payment |
| Clickable screens | A task in a realistic interface with controlled data; observe completion, mistakes and recovery | Backend quality, natural repeat use or market demand |
| Video demonstration | Show a workflow; test accurate understanding and any specific follow-up action offered | Operating the product; watching is not use |
| Wizard of Oz | A human operates behind an apparently working interface; observe the delivered experience | Feasibility, latency or cost of future automation |
| Concierge | Customer knows people deliver the service manually; observe actual use, useful outcomes, return requests and effort | Value independent of personal attention or automated delivery |
| Thin functional prototype | Real logic needed to answer a technical/interaction question; measure performance on relevant inputs | Production readiness, scale or sustained demand |

Wizard of Oz hides manual operation in the experience; concierge makes it visible. Do not describe concierge as pretending the system works. For Wizard of Oz define operator responses, study framing and handling of hidden operation. Record human assistance consistently, since it can create the apparent value.

## 2. Demand tests

Smoke test is an umbrella for testing an offer before full delivery; a fake door is one possible smoke test. Record offer, traffic source, eligible audience, exposure and action separately.

| Subtype | Setup / observed signal | Does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| Advertising smoke test | Defined offer/audience and controlled spend; eligible visits or qualified actions relative to exposures/cost | Product use, delivery value or purchase |
| Fake door | Feature entry point in the relevant workflow, with appropriate unavailable/pilot message after activation; unique eligible activations / unique eligible exposures | Task success, satisfaction, repeat use or willingness to pay |
| Landing page | Explain an offer and request a defined action, such as eligible pilot application; qualified requests / eligible visits | Delivered value; waitlist registration is not a sale |
| Pricing page | Actual package, price and terms; record package selection, checkout start, request and payment separately | Payment from selecting a price or starting checkout |

Match the action to the assumption. An ad click can be a poor substitute for a rare B2B purchasing decision. Low traffic or inaccessible users can make demand tests slower or less informative; time pressure does not make fake doors universally appropriate.

## 3. Commitment tests

State the exact ask, who can make it, its cost and reversibility. Signals differ in meaning; their strength depends on terms and context.

| Subtype | Concrete ask and signal | Limits / required distinctions |
|---|---|---|
| Letter of intent (LOI) | Decision maker signs scope, terms and intended next action | Binding status, budget authority, timing; nonbinding LOI is not guaranteed revenue |
| Saved payment method | Buyer authorizes saving a method for a stated future use | Consent/future-charge terms; setup is not a specific purchase or collected money |
| Paid reservation or deposit | Buyer pays a stated amount to reserve a defined deliverable | Refundability, remaining balance, delivery/cancellation terms; refundable deposit is not a full-price sale |
| Presale | Buyer orders a defined future deliverable at a stated price | Collected payment vs order intent, fulfillment and refund terms; not satisfaction |
| Reward crowdfunding | Backers pledge/pay for a defined reward | Pledged vs charged vs retained funds, campaign conditions, delivery costs, atypical audience |
| Paid pilot / paid concierge | Buyer pays for a bounded pilot with a clear deliverable | Scope, payer, price, fulfillment and manual costs; label prototype + commitment if both apply |
| Paid continuation / renewal | Buyer pays again after experiencing delivery and having another need | Eligible renewal opportunities, period and terms; repeat payment and use remain separate |

Do not collect raw card details in research materials. Design the appropriate payment-provider flow where relevant; distinguish setup, authorization, capture, refund and net payment. Unknown price or terms make a payment test conditional.

## Make the choice reviewable

Name the recommended assumption, family/subtype and behavior. Compare one or two plausible alternatives; do not enumerate the catalog for every concept.

- Comprehension: compare interpretations with the same underlying information. Fake doors answer a different question.
- Useful delivery: observe the intended task. Video can explain value but cannot demonstrate experienced value.
- Willingness to pay: a real, deliverable offer to an appropriate buyer. An unpaid pilot measures something else.
- Repeat use: distinguish another need from no opportunity. One session does not establish retention.

Choose the least expensive design that can discriminate the assumption. Qualitative stopping rules guide a small pilot, not population conversion. For quantitative comparisons justify sample size from the decision, baseline, meaningful effect and exposure; there are no universal conversion thresholds or sample counts.
