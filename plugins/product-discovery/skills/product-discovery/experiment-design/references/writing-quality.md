# Natural titles and descriptions

Write in the user's language from the start. A title and its short description should let a reader picture what happens without knowing discovery terminology or opening another file. Prefer a slightly longer, concrete sentence to a compact but opaque label. Do not impose one grammatical pattern on every title.

## Name the actual situation

- Name the person, what they receive or do, and the change or question that matters. Use the concrete object from the project: a delivery date, an example of completed work, an invitation, or a purchase. A role such as recruiter or shopper is sufficient; names and invented details are unnecessary.
- Do not replace those objects with bare abstractions such as evidence, signal, assessment, mechanism, or value. If a specialist term is needed, explain what it means in this situation. Replacing one abstract noun with a synonym does not clarify the action.
- Avoid labels that only make sense next to another card: "the material", "the explanation", "the card", "the result", or an internal ID. Establish what each refers to inside the same preview. IDs remain useful metadata, not descriptions.
- State only the behavior the method can actually examine. Understanding an application, judging a person's skills, deciding to invite them, and hiring them are different actions. Do not make a test sound more consequential to simplify its name.

## Explain what people will actually see and do

In a short experiment description, say who prepares or delivers the proposed change when that affects its meaning, who receives it, what differs from the existing experience, and what the participant does next. For a comparison, describe both experiences in ordinary sentences. Say what we will watch or compare, including whose behavior counts. Use research labels after this explanation when useful.

Do not compress a description into phrases such as "same material with and without explanation; assessments split between participants". The reader still cannot picture the material, the explanation's author, or the task. Shorten logistics and background first, preserving those concrete details. A few clear sentences are better than a fragment requiring a follow-up explanation.

## Illustrative wording

These examples demonstrate language only. They do not prescribe a product, method, selection, metric, or success threshold.

- Opaque title: "Does additional temporal information improve purchase assessment?"
- Descriptive title: "Will showing the delivery date help shoppers decide whether to buy?"
- Opaque method: "Compare the same material with and without additional context."
- Descriptive method: "One group of shoppers sees a product page with an estimated delivery date. Another sees the same page without that date. We compare whether they start checkout and ask each group when they expect the order to arrive."

For a payment test, describe the actual offer and payment action. For a task test, describe the task and what the person must work out. Do not turn all experiments into the comparison used in the example.

## Read the title and preview on their own

Before saving the canonical explanation, then again after shortening it for a tree or HTML card, check:

1. Can a reader say who does what, with which specific information, offer, or product change?
2. Can they picture what a participant sees and does without expanding another node?
3. Is it clear what we will observe and what this observation can tell us?
4. Does the wording sound like something a person would say aloud in the user's language, rather than a translated research label?

Rewrite unclear wording at its source, then project the same meaning into all views. A source link provides detail; it must not be necessary to understand the proposal. Preserve evidence, uncertainty, IDs, selections, methods, comparison groups, and measurement scope. Do not invent operational details to make the sentence sound concrete; name a consequential unknown explicitly.
