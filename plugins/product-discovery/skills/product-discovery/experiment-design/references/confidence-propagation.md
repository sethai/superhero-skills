# Evidence and Assumption Confidence

Keep confidence tied to a specific claim and its sources. Confidence in a customer need is distinct from confidence that a proposed solution will help.

## Evidence ratings

- **HIGH:** direct, relevant observations corroborated across independent contexts; frequency/severity claims have an appropriate basis and material contradictions are addressed.
- **MED:** relevant direct evidence with limited coverage, or corroborated indirect evidence; explicitly identify which parts remain inferred.
- **LOW:** tentative interpretation, a single uncorroborated context, substantial missing information or conflicting evidence that prevents a firm conclusion.

These are qualitative judgments, not probabilities or source-count formulas. Explain the rating. Several quotes from one person, or several people describing the same event, do not establish prevalence. Use MED consistently in matrix labels.

## Carry uncertainty forward

1. Cite the evidence for each opportunity and mark its coverage and limitations. Do not silently turn a tentative interpretation into an accepted finding.
2. For each concept, separate supported need from untested mechanism, effect size, adoption, payment and feasibility assumptions. Strong evidence of pain does not validate the solution.
3. In experiment cards, link the assumption to its evidence and remaining gap. A design does not itself increase confidence; actual observations may change a particular claim within the scope of the sample and method.
4. Pending human reviews stay pending until the user accepts them. New sources can revise an interpretation, with the change and counterevidence recorded.

Do not assign the minimum confidence of every nearby observation to an entire cluster. Identify which claims depend on uncertain evidence, and carry those specific limitations forward. Do not improve a rating merely by adding more quotes.

## Present proportionate limitations

Say what is missing and how it affects the decision, for example: "This need appears in two organizations, but no one has used the proposed workflow." When evidence is thin, compare further research with running a prototype; neither is automatically required. Keep limitations visible without repeating generic warning banners in every section.
